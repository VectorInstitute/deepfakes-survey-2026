Deepfake Detection Beyond the Lab

A Survey of Real-World Distribution, Forensics, and Provenance

Abstract

Deepfake detection is usually measured on curated benchmarks, yet most synthetic video reaches an audience through social media platforms that compress, resize, re-encode, crop, and repost it long before any detector sees it. The practical question is therefore not whether a detector separates real from fake under laboratory conditions, but whether the evidence it depends on still exists at the moment a decision has to be made.

This survey reviews 239 works on generative audio-video deepfakes across the full lifecycle of creation, platform distribution, detection, provenance, and remediation. We organize the review around five forensic assumptions that detectors implicitly rely on: that a manipulated region leaves a boundary, that a generator leaves a stable fingerprint, that test data resemble training data, that forensic signals survive processing, and that low-level clues are sufficient.

Using these assumptions as a common lens, we present an evidence-based taxonomy of generation methods; review signal-driven, learning-based, reasoning-based, agentic, and adversarially robust detection; and examine provenance mechanisms including content credentials and watermarking alongside emerging disclosure and takedown regulation.

Two findings recur. First, foundation-model generation invalidates several assumptions at once, since fully synthetic and jointly generated audio-video content offers neither a compositing boundary nor an exploitable cross-modal inconsistency. Second, the literature is concentrated on creation and detection, with distribution, provenance, and remediation together accounting for 12% of the corpus. We close with evaluation protocols that report which assumptions a benchmark actually exercises.

The Evidence Gap

Roughly 5.24 billion social media identities were active at the start of 2025. A deepfake is not simply a video to classify: it passes through upload, transcoding, recommendation, reposting, review, and eventual removal or continued spread. Every one of those steps rewrites the forensic record.

Our corpus makes the imbalance explicit. Creation and detection account for 75% of the 239 reviewed works, while distribution, provenance, and remediation together account for 12% — the stages that determine what evidence survives to a detector are the stages the field has studied least. Cumulative generation research overtook detection research by 24 works in 2024.

Positioning

Prior surveys concentrate on generation and detection taxonomies, reliability, proactive defense, or model architectures. Few connect forensic detection with the platform conditions under which synthetic media is processed, propagated, verified, and remediated.

This survey joins those strands, combining generative foundations and forensic detection with a social-media lifecycle perspective, and adds agentic detection — largely absent elsewhere.

Five Forensic Assumptions

Detectors rely on assumptions about the evidence available to them. We group these expectations into five assumptions, A1–A5, which form the analytical spine of the survey. Each section of the paper states which assumptions the methods it reviews depend on, and which ones fail.

A1

Boundary Artifact

A manipulated region leaves a boundary.

  • Compositing seams
  • Texture & colour mismatch
  • Resolution discontinuity
  • Local compression shift

Fails when video is fully synthetic — every pixel is generated, so there is nothing to composite against.

A2

Generator Fingerprint

A generator leaves a stable fingerprint.

  • Upsampling traces
  • Spectral signatures
  • VAE-decoder bias
  • Vocoder residuals

Fails when generators are chained, fine-tuned, quantized, or distilled — no single fingerprint survives the chain.

A3

Distribution Alignment

Test videos resemble the training data.

  • Cross-generator shift
  • Cross-manipulation shift
  • Cross-quality shift
  • Cross-domain shift

Fails when new generators, identities, languages, or platforms appear after training — which is the default in deployment.

A4

Signal Survival

Forensic signals survive processing.

  • Transcoding
  • Resizing & cropping
  • Re-encoding chains
  • Screen recording

Fails when media passes through platform pipelines: the smallest edited regions degrade earliest.

A5

Low-Level Sufficiency

Low-level clues are enough.

  • Pixels & textures
  • Frequency statistics
  • Frame-level motion
  • Cross-modal mismatch

Fails when audio and video are generated jointly — the two channels agree by construction, so mismatch is no longer evidence.

Reading the A1–A5 labels. They mean different things in different tables. For generation, they report whether an assumption holds for a method's outputs. For detection, whether a method depends on an assumption or mitigates its failure. For datasets, which assumptions a benchmark can stress-test.

The Deepfake Attack Pipeline

We formalize deepfake video creation as a five-stage pipeline. Every stage adds, alters, or suppresses forensic traces. The data cost of a convincing target has collapsed: from a corpus of identity-specific frames, to a single reference image with training-free identity injection, to none at all for untargeted text-to-video generation.

Generation Taxonomy

We organize the generation literature by manipulation target, because the target determines what forensic evidence remains available to a detector. The assumptions fail in order: A1 goes with the seam, A2 outlives it but fails wherever generators are chained, and A5 is what jointly generated audio and video remove.

Detection Families

We review detection through the same forensic lens. Each family buys a different kind of evidence, and each inherits a different failure mode.

1

Signal-Driven Forensics

Face X-Ray, ID-Reveal, frequency analysis, AEROBLADE, LipForensics; RawNet2 and AASIST for acoustic; codec and compression history.

Depends on A1, A2, A4, A5

2

Learning-Based Architectures

CNN (SBI, LAA-Net), transformer (TALL, AltFreezing), foundation-adapted (UnivFD, Forensics Adapter, FSFM), fully synthetic (UNITE, DeMamba).

A3 dominant

3

Generalization & Adaptation

Data-, model-, and learning-level strategies. Deepfake-Eval-2024 measures roughly 50% AUC loss for open-source video detectors on in-the-wild media.

A3 cannot be removed

4

Reasoning-Based & Agentic

Zero-shot VLMs; knowledge-grounded FakeShield, Veritas, Omni-Fake-R1; agentic AIFo and Agent4FaceForgery.

Substitutes for A5

5

Adversarial Robustness

Learned anti-forensics that strips fingerprints; prompt injection against reasoning detectors; evaluation after platform-representative processing.

A2 and A3 are attackable

6

Operating Points

At 0.1% prevalence, a detector with 90% TPR and 1% FPR reaches only ~8.3% precision. Calibration and abstention matter more than AUC.

Prevalence governs deployed precision

The Social Media Lifecycle

The lifecycle perspective shifts deepfake analysis from a static-file problem to an evolving-evidence problem: the central question is not only whether content is fake, but what evidence remains when a decision must be made.

Provenance & Content Authenticity

C2PA manifests bind signed metadata to an asset; in-generation watermarking (Stable Signature, Tree-Ring, Gaussian Shading) and post-hoc watermarking (VideoSeal, RivaGAN, AudioSeal, WavMark) embed recoverable signals.

Both inherit familiar limits: an embedded watermark must survive compression and re-encoding (A4), and recovery may depend on a specific decoder or generator family (A2). Removal attacks erase marks; forgery attacks apply them to authentic media.

Governance & Remediation

EU AI Act Articles 50(2) and 50(4), China's deep-synthesis provisions, South Korea's election rules, the U.S. TAKE IT DOWN Act, and measures in Australia, the UK, and Canada.

Marking obligations bind identifiable providers. Open-weight generators fall outside them — and that is the segment most often used to produce targeted non-consensual and political content. Unaided human review is near chance.

Recommended protocol. Go beyond a single random split: report an in-domain baseline plus cross-dataset or cross-generator evaluation; add realistic transformations (compression, resizing, re-encoding, noise, cropping); separate audio-only, video-only, and joint manipulations so performance cannot be attributed to a shortcut in one modality; and state which of A1–A5 the benchmark actually exercises.

Key Findings

1. Foundation-model generation invalidates several assumptions at once

Contemporary generators leave no compositing boundary and no stable fingerprint (A1, A2), come from architectures too varied for paradigm-specific detectors (A3), and synthesize audio and video jointly, so the two channels agree (A5). Fully synthetic and jointly generated content offers neither a boundary nor an exploitable cross-modal inconsistency.

2. The stages that determine what evidence survives are the least studied

Distribution, provenance, and remediation together account for 12% of the corpus, against 75% for creation and detection. Table 5 shows that most platform effects are asserted rather than measured: no reviewed work reports time-to-detection, posts before detection, or platforms reached for deployed detection.

3. No single defense covers the space

Passive detection fails under evasion; watermarking under removal and open-weight non-participation; signed provenance under credential stripping; attribution under multi-stage pipelines. Provenance coverage is bounded by the compliant share of the generator population, not by watermark robustness — leaving passive detection as the only evidence for exactly the segment most often used to produce targeted non-consensual and political content.

4. Benchmark scores do not transfer to circulated media

Detectors reported near ceiling on curated benchmarks lose roughly half their AUC on contemporary circulated media. Benchmarks must be refreshed as generators are released, and each should state which assumptions it exercises.

Open Challenges

1

Generalization Beyond Known Generators

Open-set and unknown-source handling rather than closed-set benchmarking. Generator-agnostic representations, authentic-data modelling, continual adaptation.

2

Fingerprint Stability Across Lineages

Report attribution over checkpoint families and generation chains rather than over single released models.

3

Foundation-Model & Joint AV Forensics

Coordinated generation removes cross-modal inconsistency as free evidence. Which signals remain transferable?

4

Detection Across the Lifecycle

Couple passive detection with provenance, context, calibrated uncertainty, and human review — without letting one unreliable component dominate.

5

Living Benchmarks

Continuously refreshed corpora in which newly released generators appear only after detector training; calibrated scores at realistic prevalence.

6

Deployment Evaluation

Measure calibration, abstention, time to detection, and performance after realistic platform transformations — not a single AUC.
